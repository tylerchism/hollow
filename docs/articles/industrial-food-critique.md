# The Efficiency Trap: Dismantling the Industrial Food Apologia

I read this piece in the Times, and I have to say, it is a masterclass in missing the forest for the trees. The author—and this is a very common perspective in economics and industrial food policy—is operating from a framework that prioritizes calories and shelf-life as the ultimate metrics of a food system, while treating nutrient density and biological compatibility as optional luxuries for the rich.

If you look at this through the lens of Weston A. Price, or even just basic nutritional biochemistry, the entire foundation of this article collapses.

Let's go through this, because the framing here sets up a false dichotomy that is actually quite dangerous if you're trying to optimize your health.

## The "Efficiency" Fallacy

"The industrial food system is a miracle of efficiency, producing more calories per acre than any organic farm could dream of, thereby keeping prices low for the working class."

This is the central pillar of their argument, right? It sounds logical on the surface: Industrial = Efficient = Cheap = Good for the Poor.

But we need to ask: Efficient at producing what?

If the goal is to produce the maximum amount of glucose and seed oils per acre to fuel a blast furnace, then yes, the industrial system is efficient. But the human body isn't a blast furnace; it's a biochemical machine that requires roughly 30 to 40 essential micronutrients to run its enzymatic pathways.

When the author says "efficiency," they are talking about the production of macronutrient mass—starch, sugar, and fat—stripped of the micronutrient context that makes those calories usable by the body.

Weston Price called these the "displacing foods of modern commerce." When he traveled the globe, he didn't find that populations eating traditional, local diets were starving because they lacked "industrial efficiency." He found they were robust, immune to dental decay, and highly fertile. It was only when the "efficient" foods—white flour, sugar, canned goods—arrived that you saw the physical degeneration.

So, the article frames this as "Industrial vs. Hunger." The real framing should be "Industrial Caloric Abundance vs. Cellular Starvation." We are feeding the world, sure, but we are feeding them into a state of metabolic dysfunction because we've optimized for energy storage rather than energy utilization.

## The "Elitism" Trap

"Ideally, we'd all eat local kale, but insisting on organic, local food is an elitist position that ignores the economic realities of most families."

This is a rhetorical sleight of hand that drives me nuts.

The author is suggesting that the inherent cost of real food is high, and therefore we need industrial sludge to feed the poor. But they are ignoring why the industrial food is cheap.

It is cheap because we have constructed a subsidy system that artificially lowers the cost of corn, soy, and wheat—the raw materials of processed food—while imposing massive regulatory burdens on the local producer of meat, milk, and vegetables.

If you look at the "foods of modern commerce" Price warned about, they are shelf-stable. Why? Because the nutrients that cause spoilage—like omega-3 fatty acids, or enzymes in raw milk—have been removed or destroyed. The "efficiency" of the industrial system is largely the efficiency of removing life from the food so it can sit in a warehouse for six months.

When you buy that "cheap" industrial food, you are paying a hidden tax. You might save $2 at the checkout counter, but you pay it back with interest in the form of mitochondrial dysfunction, dental bills, and chronic disease management later. True elitism isn't saying "eat real food"; true elitism is a system that makes the foods required for human flourishing accessible only to the wealthy, while telling the poor they should be grateful for fortified refined flour.

## The "Fortification" Argument (The Iodine Issue)

"Industrial processing allows us to fortify foods, eliminating goiters with iodized salt and pellagra with enriched flour—public health wins that local food systems often struggle to replicate."

This touches on exactly what we were discussing regarding the trade-offs of leaving the Standard American Diet.

The author is right about one narrow thing: The industrial system is very good at patching the holes that it created.

Pellagra (vitamin B3 deficiency) became an epidemic in the South specifically because industrial milling removed the niacin from corn and wheat. Their solution wasn't "let's go back to traditional preparation methods" (like nixtamalization, which makes niacin bioavailable); it was "let's synthetically add niacin back in."

It's the same with iodine. We moved away from nutrient-dense seafood and organ meats and towards a diet of muscle meat and grain, so we lost our iodine source. The industrial "fix" is iodized salt.

But here is the biochemical rub: Nutrients don't work in isolation.

When you fortify flour with folic acid, you aren't getting the folate in its natural food matrix, and you certainly aren't getting the betaine, choline, and B12 that would naturally accompany it in liver or eggs. You are getting a synthetic isolate that interacts differently with your physiology.

The article frames this as, "Industrial food gave us safety." I would frame it as, "Industrial food broke our metabolism, and fortification is the band-aid keeping us alive enough to keep buying the food."

If you switch to a local/organic diet, yes, you lose the safety net of fortification (like the iodine in salt). But the answer isn't to run back to the industrial system; the answer is to design your local diet with the same wisdom traditional cultures did. You don't need iodized salt if you are eating clams, oysters, or fish heads. You don't need enriched flour if you are eating liver.

## The "Natural is a Fallacy" Argument

"Cyanide is natural; preservatives are safe. We should trust the food scientists who ensure our food doesn't kill us with botulism."

This is a classic straw man. The author is conflating acute toxicity (botulism) with chronic degeneration (metabolic syndrome).

Of course we want to avoid botulism. But we didn't need sodium benzoate and BHT to survive for the last 200,000 years. We used fermentation, salting, drying, and smoking—traditional processing methods that often enhanced nutrient bioavailability (think of sauerkraut having more Vitamin C than raw cabbage).

Industrial preservatives allow us to consume ultra-processed combinations of fat and sugar that bypass our natural satiety signals. The author argues that preservatives prevent waste. I would argue that preservatives allow the creation of "foods" that are hyper-palatable and completely uncoupled from nutritional value.

The brain has a specific executive control over energy balance. It is looking for nutrients. If you feed it a preservative-laden, nutrient-poor industrial product, the brain says, "I have plenty of calories, but where is the magnesium? Where is the protein? Keep eating."

## The Verdict: What Question Are We Actually Asking?

This article is fundamentally asking the wrong question. It asks: "How do we get the most calories to the most people for the least money?"

The question we should be asking, the question Weston Price asked, is: "What are the biological requirements for a human to form a healthy body and reproduce?"

The industrial system described in this article fails that second question completely. It provides energy, but it fails to support the infrastructure of our energy metabolism.

The author makes a compelling economic case for 2026, but physiologically, they are advocating for a system that manages sickness rather than creates health. And the failure runs deeper than economics. The historical framing the article relies on is doing just as much rhetorical work as the efficiency argument—and it's just as wrong.

## The "Turn of the Century" Fallacy

"At the turn of the 20th century, when our great-great-grandparents had no choice but to eat 'real food,' malnutrition was rampant."

This is the most critical sleight of hand in the entire article. By choosing the year 1900 as the baseline for "the past," the author is not pointing to an ancestral human diet; they are pointing to the very peak of the nutritional transition that Weston A. Price warned against.

By 1900 in the West, the industrial revolution had already radically altered the food supply. "Real food" had effectively vanished for the urban working class. They were not eating the nutrient-dense, organ-meat-rich, fermented, fresh diets of their ancestors. They were eating the first generation of shelf-stable industrial commodities: refined white flour, refined sugar, canned meats, and vegetable oils (which were just entering the supply).

The "rampant malnutrition" the author cites—rickets, pellagra, scurvy—were not diseases of "the past"; they were diseases of modernization. Pellagra, for instance, exploded in the American South after the introduction of industrial milling techniques that removed the germ from corn. The author uses the failures of early industrial food (refined but not yet fortified) to justify the existence of late industrial food (refined and chemically fortified).

True "real food"—the diets of the hunter-gatherers, pastoralists, and traditional agriculturalists that Price studied—did not produce these deficiencies. When Price visited the isolated Swiss in the Loetschental Valley or the Gaelic communities in the Outer Hebrides in the 1930s, they were eating "real food," and they had virtually no tooth decay, wide dental arches, and high resistance to tuberculosis. The malnutrition the article references is actually the result of the loss of those traditions, not adherence to them.

## The "Edenic Fantasy" Strawman

"...fantasy of Edenic premodern food that never existed."

This is a rhetorical device designed to pathologize anyone who questions the industrial system. It implies that the only alternative to the supermarket is a magical delusion where no one ever died.

We don't need to appeal to an "Edenic fantasy"; we can look at the skeletal record.

When anthropologists compare skeletal remains from pre-agricultural (hunter-gatherer) populations to post-agricultural (and especially post-industrial) ones, the difference is stark. Pre-agricultural humans generally had robust bone density, wide dental arches with room for all 32 teeth (no wisdom tooth impaction), and very little evidence of dental caries (cavities).

The "Eden" the author mocks is simply biological fitness. The shift to industrial agriculture brought about a measurable physical degeneration: a shrinking of the jaw, a narrowing of the face, and a susceptibility to metabolic disease. The author conflates "surviving acute infectious disease" (which modern medicine is good at) with "thriving without chronic degeneration" (which the modern food system is terrible at). Pretending that widespread dental rot and metabolic dysfunction are the price we must pay for not starving is a false dichotomy.

## The Defense of Ultra-Processed Foods

"Research has also shown that many ultra processed foods... are not linked to worse health outcomes and may even be beneficial."

This assertion relies on a reductionist view of nutrition—often called "nutrientism"—where food is nothing more than a delivery vehicle for specific chemical compounds.

When studies show UPFs are "beneficial," they are almost always comparing a fortified processed food against a non-fortified starvation diet in a specific context. For example, if you take a population that is deficient in fiber and give them a fiber-enriched processed snack bar, their biomarkers might improve in the short term compared to the control.

However, this ignores the food matrix. A synthetic vitamin added to a refined grain does not behave the same way in the body as that vitamin bound to proteins and fats in a whole food. Furthermore, this defense ignores the mechanism of satiety. Ultra-processed foods are engineered to be hyper-palatable, bypassing the brain's "stop" signals (a concept known as the Protein Leverage Hypothesis suggests we overeat energy to meet our protein needs when food is diluted with refined carbs/fats).

To claim these foods are "beneficial" is to lower the bar of human health to "survival." It frames the absence of acute deficiency (like scurvy) as health, while ignoring the massive rise in chronic, non-communicable diseases (diabetes, heart disease) that correlates perfectly with the rise of these very foods.

## The Synthesis

The article successfully argues against a ghost. It argues that we shouldn't return to the squalor of 1900s tenement life—a point on which everyone agrees. But it uses that fear to dismiss the legitimate anthropological evidence that humans can, and did, thrive on local, nutrient-dense food systems for thousands of years.

The industrial system didn't save us from the inherent dangers of "real food"; it created a new set of problems (nutrient stripping) which it then "solved" with a profitable technological patch (fortification/preservatives), creating a dependency loop that the author now mistakes for salvation. Don't fall for the "efficiency" trap. The most efficient thing you can do is give your mitochondria the nutrients they need to work, and you usually won't find those in a box of "fortified" industrial commodity crops.
